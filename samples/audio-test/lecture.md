# Audience Analysis in Business Communication

## Why audience analysis comes first

Every piece of business writing fails or succeeds on one question: did the writer know who was
reading it? A proposal that is technically perfect and aimed at the wrong reader is a failed
proposal. The order of work is therefore fixed: analyse the audience, then decide the message, then
choose the channel, and only then write. Writers who reverse the first two steps produce documents
that describe what they know rather than what the reader needs.

There are four audiences for most business documents, and they rarely read the same parts.

The **primary audience** is the person who will act on the document — approve the budget, sign the
contract, change the process. They read the recommendation and the cost.

The **secondary audience** is asked to comment before the primary audience decides. A finance
analyst checking the numbers is a secondary audience. They read the method.

The **gatekeeper** controls whether the document reaches the primary audience at all. An executive
assistant, a compliance reviewer, and a manager who forwards or does not forward are all
gatekeepers. They read the subject line and the first paragraph, and nothing else.

The **watchdog audience** has no power over the decision but has a stake in it and may act later:
a regulator, a journalist, a union representative, a competitor. They read for what was left out.

A document written only for the primary audience routinely dies at the gatekeeper.

## The three questions

Audience analysis answers three questions, in this order.

**What does the reader already know?** This sets the vocabulary and the amount of background. The
common error is to assume less knowledge than the reader has, which reads as condescension, or more
than they have, which reads as evasion. A reliable test: if a term would need a definition in a
first meeting, define it in the document.

**What does the reader need to do?** Every business document asks for something — a decision, a
signature, an action, or simply awareness. If the writer cannot state the requested action in one
sentence, the reader will not find it either.

**What will make the reader resist?** Cost, effort, risk to reputation, and loss of control are the
four standard sources of resistance. Naming the objection before the reader raises it is more
persuasive than answering it afterwards, because it demonstrates that the writer considered it.

## Direct and indirect organisation

Business writing uses two organising patterns, and the choice between them is an audience decision,
not a stylistic one.

**Direct organisation** puts the main point first, then the support. Use it when the news is good or
neutral, when the reader is busy, when the reader already agrees, or when the document is routine.
Most internal business writing should be direct. A reader who has to hunt for the point will often
stop hunting.

**Indirect organisation** puts the reasoning first and the main point after it. Use it when the news
is bad, when the reader is likely to resist, or when the writer needs to establish credibility
before making a claim. The reasoning is not padding: it is the evidence the reader needs before the
conclusion will be accepted rather than rejected.

The failure mode of direct organisation is bluntness. The failure mode of indirect organisation is
burial — the reader never reaches the point. A useful rule: indirect organisation may delay the main
point, but never past the first page.

## Channel selection

Channel is chosen on two axes: richness and permanence.

**Richness** is how much information a channel carries beyond the words — tone, expression, the
chance to ask a question immediately. A face-to-face conversation is the richest channel; a posted
notice is the leanest. Rich channels suit ambiguous, emotional, or contested messages. Lean channels
suit routine, factual ones.

**Permanence** is whether the message leaves a record. Email and written memos are permanent and
forwardable. Conversations are not. Permanence is an advantage when a decision needs a trail, and a
liability when the message is sensitive.

The standard error is using a lean permanent channel — email — for a rich impermanent message, such
as delivering criticism. The message is misread because tone is absent, and it is forwarded because
it is permanent.

## The you-attitude

The you-attitude means writing from the reader's perspective rather than the writer's. It is not
simply using the word "you". "You failed to submit your timesheet" uses the word and abandons the
attitude.

Compare: "We require all timesheets by Friday so we can process payroll." Now: "Submitting your
timesheet by Friday means your pay arrives on time." Both sentences convey the same deadline. The
second states the reader's benefit, which is the only reason the reader has to act.

The you-attitude is strongest in requests and weakest in bad news, where emphasising the reader's
perspective can read as false sympathy. In bad-news messages, precision is kinder than warmth.

## Measuring readability

Two practical measures are used in business settings.

**Sentence length.** Average business sentences run 15 to 20 words. Comprehension falls sharply
above 25 words per sentence, because readers lose the subject before reaching the verb.

**Paragraph length.** A paragraph in business writing carries one idea and runs four to six lines on
screen. Longer paragraphs are not read; they are skimmed, and skimming loses the qualifications that
make a statement accurate.

Neither measure is a goal in itself. They are symptoms: long sentences usually mean the writer had
not decided what the sentence was about.

## What gets documents rejected

In review after review, the same four faults account for most rejected business documents.

The requested action is not stated, or is stated only at the end. The cost is presented without a
comparison, so the reader has no way to judge whether it is high. The risks are omitted, which a
watchdog audience reads as concealment. And the document answers the question the writer found
interesting rather than the question the reader asked.

All four are audience failures rather than writing failures, which is why audience analysis is the
first step and not the last.
